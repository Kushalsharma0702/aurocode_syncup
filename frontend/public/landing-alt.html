The problem

Feedback arrives broken. Then you pay to reassemble it.

Every project that ships visual work for a non-technical buyer loses hours to the same three things.

Translation loss

A client describes a problem in words. You spend real time working out which page, which element, which screen size, which state. Every screenshot is a small decoding job.

“The thing at the top looks squished on my phone”

Scattered across five apps

Feedback forks the moment two people reply. Nothing is anchored to the live page, so version 3 arrives through a different channel than version 2 — and gets missed.

WhatsAppEmailVoice noteScreenshot.png Phone callSlack“as discussed”

Status anxiety

The client can't see progress, so they ask. Asking costs you a context switch; not answering costs them confidence. Both of you dislike the Friday update email.

“Just checking in — any update on this?”
How it works

One loop, and it closes where the client can see it

Feedback tools capture well but go quiet afterwards. Client portals have nothing happening inside them. SyncUp is the loop that joins the two.

01

They point

Your client clicks the element that's wrong on their own live site and types one sentence. A screenshot, the page URL and the exact click position attach themselves.

Client · 10 seconds
02

It becomes work

It arrives in your board as a real task, tagged as feedback — not an email you have to re-type. You get notified; they're told when to expect an update.

SyncUp · instant
03

You fix it

Work it like any other task. When you mark it done, the client is told it's fixed and live — with a link straight back to the thing they pointed at.

You · in your own time
04

They confirm

The client checks and taps Looks fixed — or reopens it if it isn't right. The loop closes in front of them, which is the part that builds trust.

Client · one tap

See it work

Three moments, start to finish

Press play on any of them. Turn sound on in the top bar for the full effect.

01 · Client files it

They click. They type. Done.

No account, no app, no “can you send a screenshot”. The widget captures the page, masks anything they've typed into a form, and sends it.

02 · You get real work

It lands as a task, not an email

Screenshot, page, click position and browser all attached. Feedback is counted separately from planned scope, so bug reports never drag your progress bar down.

03 · The loop closes

“Fixed and live — have a look”

They get a message, check it, and confirm. Or reopen it. Either way you both know exactly where it stands, and there's a record of it.

Who it helps

Both sides get the thing they actually want

The client wants to be heard and kept informed. You want fewer interruptions and a record of what was asked for.

Your client

Non-technical · busy · lives in WhatsApp
  • → Never creates an account. One private link, sent over WhatsApp or email, signs them straight in.
  • → Points instead of describing. No more hunting for the words for “that bit at the top”.
  • → Knows where things stand. Received → In progress → Fixed, on the thing they reported.
  • → Gets told when to expect news the moment they submit, so they don't have to chase.
  • → Can say “looks good” on a page that's fine — not every message has to be a complaint.

You, the studio

Solo dev or small team · 4–6 live projects
  • → Feedback arrives usable. Screenshot, URL, click position and browser, every time.
  • → Fewer “any update?” messages, because the answer is already in front of them.
  • → Every request timestamped. When scope creeps, you have the receipts rather than a memory.
  • → Progress stays honest. Client bug reports don't make a healthy project look like it's slipping.
  • → You look like a company, not a person with an inbox.
